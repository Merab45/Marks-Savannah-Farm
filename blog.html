Livestock

Choosing cattle for your farm goals

Begin by deciding what role cattle should play in your enterprise: milk, beef, breeding, or a combination. That choice affects the type of animal, feed plan, housing, labour, and the buyers you need to reach.

Before you buy

  • Set a budget that includes transport, feed, veterinary care, and the first months of management.
  • Ask for the animal’s age, health history, vaccination and treatment records, and production information where available.
  • Check that your water, fencing, handling space, and feed supply suit the number of animals you plan to keep.
  • Arrange a trusted livestock professional to help assess animals if you are new to buying.

Start with a scale you can manage consistently. Ask the seller what support, documentation, and delivery arrangements are available.

Poultry

Planning a poultry project before you begin

A poultry project depends on reliable daily routines. Before sourcing chicks or growers, make sure housing, water, feed, hygiene, and a route to market are ready.

Build your checklist

  • Choose a poultry type and production goal that match local demand and your experience.
  • Prepare a dry, ventilated, secure house with enough space for the flock as it grows.
  • Plan access to clean water and a dependable feed supply before stocking.
  • Set practical biosecurity rules for visitors, equipment, cleaning, and sick birds.
  • Identify likely buyers and confirm how product will be collected, stored, and delivered.

Write down expected costs and ask a qualified animal health professional for a prevention plan suited to your location.

Crop planning

Build a crop plan around your conditions

A sound crop plan starts with the field you have and the market you can reach. Rainfall, soil, slope, water access, labour, and transport all influence what is practical.

Plan the season

  • Use a soil test or local agricultural advice to understand soil needs.
  • Review recent rainfall patterns and identify how you will manage dry spells or excess water.
  • Choose seed and varieties recommended for your area and intended use.
  • Schedule land preparation, planting, weeding, and harvest around available labour.
  • Speak with buyers early about quality, volumes, timing, and delivery requirements.

Keep simple records of inputs, dates, yields, and sales. They help you compare seasons and make more informed choices.

Dairy

From milk production to a dependable customer

Quality depends on careful handling at every step. Agree on collection times and quality expectations with buyers or processors, then build a routine your team can follow every day.

Keep the chain consistent

  • Use clean, food-safe containers and follow recommended milking hygiene.
  • Keep milk away from contaminants and manage cooling or delivery promptly.
  • Maintain clear records for quantities, collection, cleaning, and any issues.
  • Confirm the buyer’s testing, packaging, collection, and payment requirements in advance.

Local dairy handling and food safety requirements may apply. Check with the relevant county offices and your buyer before selling processed products.

Farm equipment

Buy or hire? Choosing equipment that pays its way

The right equipment solves a recurring bottleneck. Compare ownership with hiring or sharing by looking beyond the purchase price.

Compare the full cost

  • Estimate realistic use per season and the value of time saved.
  • Include fuel, repairs, routine service, storage, operator skills, and transport.
  • Ask about parts availability, warranty, training, and local repair support.
  • Compare the total ownership cost with hire fees and likely availability during peak periods.
  • Check the machine’s capacity and condition against your land, crop, and workload.

If demand is occasional or uncertain, hiring can preserve cash. If equipment is used frequently and timely access matters, ownership may make sense after a careful cost comparison.

Business planning

A practical checklist for a farm enterprise

Before committing money, set out how the enterprise will work from inputs to customer. A short written plan makes assumptions visible and helps you spot gaps early.

Cover the essentials

  • Customer: Who is likely to buy, what do they require, and how will you reach them?
  • Production: What land, animals, inputs, equipment, skills, and labour are required?
  • Costs: Include start-up purchases, recurring expenses, maintenance, transport, and a reserve for setbacks.
  • Cash flow: Note when expenses fall due and when income is likely to arrive.
  • Risks: Consider weather, disease, input price changes, and buyer availability; decide how you will respond.
  • Records: Track purchases, production, sales, and payments from the start.

Use local prices and advice when preparing forecasts. Results depend on location, season, management, and market access, so treat projections as estimates rather than guarantees.
